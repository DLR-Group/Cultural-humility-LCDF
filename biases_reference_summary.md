# Part 1 — Slide 13 Reference Summary

## STIGMA BIAS

**Definition:** The provider views SUD as a moral failing rather than a medical condition. The patient is seen as lacking willpower, not as someone with a chronic brain disorder. This attitude directly shapes clinical decisions — including whether to offer or continue MOUD.

**Study — Stone et al. (2021)**
Surveyed U.S. primary care physicians about their attitudes toward OUD treatment. Found that physicians who held stigmatizing views of patients with SUD were significantly less likely to prescribe buprenorphine, even when they had the waiver to do so. The bias didn't just affect bedside manner — it directly suppressed treatment access. Published in *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*.

---

## ATTRIBUTION BIAS

**Definition:** When a provider attributes a patient's behavior to character or identity ("they're an addict") rather than circumstances or illness, it changes how they treat that person. Language in the chart encodes this — words like "abuser" signal moral judgment, not diagnosis.

**Study — Bradford et al. (2024)**
Found that patients whose charts contained the word "abuser" (vs. person-first or clinical language) were 4× less likely to have an OUD treatment plan documented. The language wasn't neutral — it signaled to subsequent providers how to categorize the patient, creating downstream treatment disparities. This connects directly to the Kelly & Westerhoff (2010) work on language in the handout.

---

## THERAPEUTIC PESSIMISM

**Definition:** The provider doesn't believe treatment will work for this patient. Often framed as "clinical realism," but it functions as a self-fulfilling prophecy — lower engagement, less follow-through on treatment planning, reduced advocacy.

**Study — Avery et al. (2019)**
Surveyed SUD counselors — the people whose entire job is to help patients recover — and found 15–26% held stigmatizing beliefs about their own patients. Notably, this wasn't just about street drugs; counselors also expressed pessimism about MOUD patients. The irony: the people delivering care carry the same cultural scripts as everyone else.

---

## MOUD-SPECIFIC BIAS

**Definition:** A subset of stigma bias directed at the medication itself. The belief that buprenorphine or methadone is "just substituting one drug for another" — meaning the patient isn't really in recovery. This is clinically wrong (MOUD reduces overdose deaths, increases retention, improves function) but culturally persistent.

**Study — Dickson-Gomez et al. (2022)**
Interviewed both treatment providers and people who use opioids and found that "you're not supposed to be on it forever" was a dominant belief on both sides. Providers internalized and transmitted this stigma, and patients absorbed it — leading some to taper off MOUD against clinical advice because they felt they weren't "really" recovering. Published in *Substance Abuse: Research and Treatment*.

---

## RACIAL & ETHNIC BIAS

**Definition:** Implicit or explicit differential treatment by race/ethnicity in clinical decisions. In MOUD specifically, this manifests as unequal access to buprenorphine (office-based, perceived as more convenient and less stigmatized) vs. methadone (clinic-based, more restrictive).

**Study — Lagisetty et al. (2019)**
Used national prescription data and found that White patients with OUD were significantly more likely to receive buprenorphine, while patients of color — especially Black and Latino patients — were disproportionately funneled to methadone clinics. This isn't just preference: it reflects provider prescribing patterns, insurance structures, and clinic locations. Published in *JAMA Psychiatry*.

---

## COMPASSION FATIGUE

**Definition:** Chronic exposure to patient suffering erodes empathic capacity. The provider becomes emotionally blunted — not out of cruelty, but exhaustion. Patients get processed rather than seen.

**Study — Bride et al. (2007)**
Surveyed SUD counselors specifically and found high rates of secondary traumatic stress and compassion fatigue. SUD work is particularly high-risk because counselors witness relapse, overdose, loss, and chronic suffering repeatedly. Fatigue leads to depersonalization — the clinical correlate of treating patients like cases. Published in *Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment*.
